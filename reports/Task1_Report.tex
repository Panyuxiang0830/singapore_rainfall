\textbf{Study objectives.} Using Singapore rainfall observations from 2017--2024, we analyse temporal and spatial patterns and develop models to predict the probability that station rainfall accumulated over the next 30 minutes exceeds a given threshold.

\textbf{Data and methods.} The eight NEA / data.gov.sg annual datasets contain 48,538,713 source records and 48,538,627 unique valid observations from 91 station IDs. Spark performs full-data cleaning and aggregation. Annual comparisons use 21 stable stations to reduce changes caused by the monitoring network.

\textbf{Main findings.} Of all valid five-minute observations, 97.08\% are dry. Eligible diurnal samples show the highest rain frequency at 16:00--17:00 Singapore time. Available-year monthly averages have the highest frequency in November and the lowest in July, with varying eligible years and stations. In the fixed panel, duration-normalized rainfall is lowest in 2019 and highest in 2021. Nearby stations generally have more strongly correlated daily rainfall; eight years alone do not establish long-term climate change.

\clearpage\section*{2. Metrics and comparable samples}
For a station and time period, let $N$ be the number of valid observations, $r_i\geq0$ the rainfall amount, $W=\sum_i\mathbf{1}(r_i>0)$ the number of wet intervals, and $E$ the expected calendar slots. We use:
\begin{align*}
\text{Coverage} &= N/E,\qquad E=288\times\text{calendar days},\\
\text{Wet frequency (\%)} &=100W/N,\\
\text{Wet-interval intensity (mm/5min)} &=\sum_i r_i/W\quad(W>0),\\
\text{Rain / observed 24h (mm)} &=288\sum_i r_i/N.
\end{align*}
Calendar denominators handle leap years. Low coverage can reflect a station operating for only part of a year. Intensity averages wet intervals only. For one station and period, normalized rainfall equals $288\times\text{frequency}/100\times\text{wet-interval intensity}$. This product generally does not hold after separate station averaging. Normalized rainfall is not an official daily or annual total and does not reconstruct missed storms.

\textbf{Fixed annual panel.} Of 91 station IDs across eight years, 21 satisfy all criteria: observations in every year, annual coverage of at least 80\% in each year, and latitude/longitude spans no larger than $0.005^\circ$. The other 70 are excluded only from fixed-panel annual comparisons; their valid observations were not deleted during cleaning. The 21 stations receive equal weights. The next page lists the station scope for each analysis.

\textbf{Monthly and diurnal samples.} Figures 2 and 4 further screen sufficiently complete months or hourly strata from the 21-station pool. Monthly summaries average stations equally, then available years equally. Each eligible year-month has 17--21 stations, and each month has 6--8 available years. Requiring the same station-month to qualify in all eight years leaves only May, June, July, September, October and December, used as an additional comparison.

\textbf{Spatial samples.} Figure 1 uses 2024, which has 70 stations. Of these, 56 have annual coverage of at least 90\% and enter map colouring; the other 14 are shown in grey. Figure 5 uses daily totals from the fixed 21 stations, requiring at least 95\% daily coverage and 365 common valid days per pair. The 80\%, 90\% and 95\% completeness thresholds are project choices.

\clearpage\section*{4. Task 2: prediction target and chronological splits}

\textbf{Prediction target.} For station $s$, prediction time $t$ and rainfall threshold $q$, the model estimates the probability that the following 30-minute accumulated rainfall strictly exceeds $q$. Historical station and neighbour features are retrieved for the requested station and time.
\[R_{s,t}^{30}=\sum_{k=1}^{6}r_{s,t+5k\,\mathrm{min}},\qquad y_{s,t}^{(q)}=\mathbf{1}(R_{s,t}^{30}>q),\qquad \hat p_q=P(y^{(q)}=1\mid x_{s,t}).\]

\textbf{Rainfall thresholds.} We use $q\in\{0,5,25\}$ mm for three targets: any rain, accumulation above 5 mm and accumulation above 25 mm. One classifier is trained for each target. Each station-time row retains one feature set and three label columns, and prediction uses the model corresponding to the input threshold.

\textbf{Label construction.} Taking timestamps as five-minute interval ends, sum the six valid rainfall readings in $(t,t+30\mathrm{min}]$ and compare the total at nine decimal places with the threshold. The current interval is excluded from future accumulation. A label is constructed only when all six future intervals have valid readings. Equality with the threshold is labelled zero.

\textbf{Historical features.} Use observations in the preceding 90 minutes with observation and stored-update times no later than prediction time. \textbf{Neighbour construction.} Choose the three closest other stations using coordinates known by prediction time. Disable a station spatial contribution if it moves more than $0.005^\circ$ from its initial location. Join only readings available as of $t$. Aggregate latest amounts and past 30-minute totals into means, maxima and available counts, plus wet fractions, mean distances and ages. Spark handles full-data time windows, features and neighbour joins; a small coordinate table supports distance calculations.

\textbf{Time encoding.} Let $h$ denote local hour including fractional minutes and $m$ denote month. Hour features are $\sin(2\pi h/24)$ and $\cos(2\pi h/24)$; month features are $\sin[2\pi(m-1)/12]$ and $\cos[2\pi(m-1)/12]$. Their four pairwise products give interactions, for eight time features in total. Cyclic encoding keeps neighbouring 23:00 and 00:00, and December and January, close in feature space.

Missing numeric values are filled with training-sample means and accompanied by missing indicators. Constant values are excluded. Final input dimensions are 162 for linear models and 73 for trees. \textbf{Three training policies.} Natural distribution assigns unit weights. Balanced weighting uses $w_+=N/(2P)$ and $w_-=N/[2(N-P)]$, giving equal class mass and overall mean weight one. Constrained SMOTE targets 20\% positives, caps their total at ten times the original positives, and augments training only. Calibration, selection and test are neither resampled nor class-weighted.

\textbf{Constrained SMOTE.} To retain relationships among station, time and historical rainfall, search up to five neighbours among minority examples with the same station, missingness, discrete states and rainfall-zero pattern. Interpolate 16 continuous rainfall features using one coefficient; time encoding, coordinates, observation counts, wet fractions and ages stay fixed. Examples without suitable neighbours are duplicated. Generated features satisfy nonnegative rainfall, ordering of complete totals and wet-count consistency. Single-thread sklearn searches training minority examples; other data processing and training use Spark.

\textbf{Probability calibration (Platt method).} After training each base classifier, fit a score-to-probability function on 2023 H1 observations: $\hat p=1/[1+\exp(-(az+b))]$, where $z$ is the raw model score and $a,b$ are fitted parameters with $a\geq0$. This converts SVM scores into probabilities and adjusts probability scales after weighting or SMOTE. The aim is agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies: for example, approximately 10\% of examples assigned about 10\% probability should be positive. The calibration function is fitted using MLlib logistic regression with regularization $10^{-6}$.
\clearpage\section*{7. Evaluation protocol and validation selection}

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.} ROC-AUC measures positive-negative ranking, with random-ranking reference 0.5. PR-AUC combines precision and recall and is useful when positives are rare. Under random ranking, precision at different recall levels approaches positive prevalence $\pi$, giving a PR-area reference near $\pi$. Test prevalences are 0.06360, 0.008345 and 0.0005517. Both ROC-AUC and PR-AUC use Spark integration over exact score ranks.
\[\mathrm{Brier}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat p_i-y_i)^2,\qquad \mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.\]

\textbf{Probability evaluation.} Brier score is mean squared error between predicted probabilities and labels; smaller is better. The baseline always predicts training-sample prevalence. Reliability plots group test examples with similar predictions and compare mean predicted probability on the horizontal axis with actual positive frequency on the vertical axis; ideal agreement is $y=x$. A validation-selected probability cutoff maximizing F1 supplies precision, recall, F1 and accuracy. This probability cutoff acts on model output and differs from the rainfall threshold $q$ defining labels.

\textbf{Classification findings.} Test classification metrics use probability cutoffs chosen on validation. At 25 mm, low F1 coexists with high accuracy because negatives dominate. We therefore compare models primarily by ROC-AUC and PR-AUC, and evaluate probability predictions using Brier score and reliability curves.

\textbf{SMOTE findings.} At 25 mm, training gains 8,334 examples; 773 change rainfall features and 6,061 are duplicates without suitable neighbours. Strict constraints leave considerable repeated information, which may explain limited gains. More real high-rainfall examples and tuned class weights or model parameters are possible improvements.

\textbf{Probability ordering across thresholds.} Separately trained targets can assign higher probability to a higher rainfall threshold. Among algorithm-specific selected models on 2024 data, violations of $\hat p_{25}\leq\hat p_5\leq\hat p_0$ occur in 1 logistic, 2,448 SVM, 528 GBT and 247 XGBoost windows, each out of 6,374,476. These counts compare three models from the same algorithm. Joint constraints on the final mixed-algorithm combination could be addressed with monotone calibration.

\textbf{Further analysis.} The same rainfall event can affect neighbouring stations, and consecutive half-hour windows overlap. Future evaluation could group by date or rainfall event to examine stability across stations, years and events.
